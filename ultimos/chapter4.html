Volume One · Chapter Four

The Man in the Mural

Ultimos

Kisaya did not move.

Her hand remained inside her jacket, closed around the disk.

Ultimos stood at the far window, exactly where he had been when he asked for it. The gray cloak made him part of the midnight dark. Only a narrow edge of white armor showed beneath it.

“Present it,” he said again.

Kisaya sat up slowly. “So you can take it?”

“So I can determine what three black mages were willing to kill to possess.”

“You already knew I had it.”

“Yes.”

“And you waited until now.”

“You were injured, pursued, and useful. The object was not attempting to escape.”

Kisaya looked at Orin.

He could think of no argument that would improve with time. “Let him see it. Seeing isn’t surrendering.”

Her expression informed him that he had volunteered something belonging to neither of them.

Nevertheless, she drew out the disk.

Milk-white stone caught the distant light of the Black Spire. The open hand within its many-rayed sun appeared between her fingers. A ring of marks too fine to read circled its face.

“Put it on the floor,” Ultimos said.

Kisaya crouched and placed it on the boards without releasing it.

Ultimos crossed the loft. He stopped over the disk, and for the first time since turning from the Spire, he became completely still.

“The seal of the White Order,” he said.

Kisaya’s fingers tightened against the stone. “The Grasping Hand.”

“That name was given by our enemies.”

“Then what does the disk do?” Orin asked.

“I do not know.”

Kisaya looked up sharply.

She withdrew her hand when pale light approached her knuckles.

Ultimos extended two fingers. Pale lines passed over the milk-white surface, entered the seven notches around its reverse, and faded without producing an answer.

Orin’s breath caught when the light touched the stone. It was only a thread, nothing like the beam in the chamber. His body did not care about the difference.

“The seal identifies the authority that claimed it,” he said. “It does not identify the object’s purpose. The same mark appeared upon infirmaries, archives, civic stores, warrants, and gates.”

“You were Grand White Mage,” Kisaya said.

“The Order was larger than one man’s memory.”

The answer seemed to displease him more than it satisfied her.

“There is no active structure I recognize,” Ultimos continued. “It may be incomplete. It may answer only to another object or place. Or it may have been made after I was sealed.”

“Tell me how the black mages acquired it. Omit nothing.”

Orin began with the roofing crew’s missing pay and the cooper’s cellar. Kisaya supplied what panic had broken into pieces: the wounded man clutching the pale disk, the bearded Warden striking him, the stone falling faceup beside his knee.

“His exact words,” Ultimos said.

“He told us to remember the mark,” Orin said. “Then he told the Warden, ‘You don’t know what you’re taking.’”

“Did he name himself?”

“No.”

“Did he bear this seal elsewhere? Clothing, jewelry, a mark upon the skin?”

“We were hiding behind casks while he bled to death,” Kisaya said. “We didn’t inspect his tailoring.”

Ultimos accepted the rebuke without visible reaction. “Continue.”

They told him how the Warden had taken the disk and how the three mages had followed the dead man’s gaze to the darkness behind the casks. They repeated the demands shouted through Bell Market: surrender anything placed in their care, repeat everything the man had said.

Then Kisaya described what they had overheard in the dye yard.

“The woman wanted district patrols,” she said. “The Warden refused. He said the recovery order kept the artifact in his custody until delivery. Only their three names were attached. Nothing could enter an ordinary ledger.”

Ultimos’s fingers flexed above the White Order seal.

“If your Order was their enemy,” Orin said, “why did they want something carrying its mark?”

“Enough to kill a man for it,” Kisaya added. “Enough to kill us for seeing it.”

“Their interest is not the most significant detail,” Ultimos said. “They concealed that interest from their own patrols.”

“They all wore the same robes,” Orin said.

“Uniform does not imply common purpose.”

Ultimos crouched. He turned the disk once without touching it, pale lines rolling it across the boards until the seven notches faced him.

“They did not merely want this object. They wanted no ordinary record that they had recovered it.”

The loft seemed colder.

“Why?” Orin asked.

“That is a more useful question than what the disk does.”

Ultimos sent it sliding back across the floor.

Kisaya caught it before it reached her knee. “You’re giving it back?”

“I instructed you to present it. I did not instruct you to surrender it.”

“Why?”

“Because you carry no active magic, and you have already demonstrated that you can conceal it. If the object contains a dormant structure, my armor may cause it to answer strongly enough for a detection spell to follow. It will be less detectable on you.”

“Not from you.”

“From everyone who matters less.”

Kisaya turned the disk once in her fingers, then returned it to the inside of her jacket.

“Keep it concealed unless we find something made to recognize it,” Ultimos said. “Do not place it against any structure bearing the Order seal without warning me first.”

“You think it opens something.”

“I think ignorance becomes dangerous when mistaken for a key.”

The disk had a name but no purpose.

Ultimos returned to the window. Kisaya lay down again without taking her hand from inside her jacket. Orin followed, though for a long while he listened to the city bells divide the darkness into hours.

Sleep found him sometime before dawn.

Orin woke to the sound of Veyr trying to pretend nothing had happened.

Cart wheels rattled over the lane below. Someone threw open a shutter and began arguing about coal. The first ovens of the Ashward coughed smoke into the pale morning, and somewhere close by, a woman called three children by three different names before receiving an answer from any of them.

Gray light entered through the broken windows. It found Kisaya asleep beneath a piece of canvas she had somehow taken most of during the night and Ultimos standing at the far window, exactly where Orin had last seen him.

Orin could not tell whether the white mage had slept.

Sleep had placed no distance between Orin and the chamber. White light still waited behind his eyelids, followed by the empty place where the witch had stood.

Kisaya stirred, checked the disk before checking her bruised temple, and covered the White Order seal as soon as it caught the weak dawn.

Without turning, Ultimos said, “We will not return to the healer.”

Orin struck his shoulder against a drying frame. It rocked once, shedding glass dust over his hair.

Kisaya stood. “Because of the searches?”

Ultimos turned from the window. His gray hood still concealed the smooth helmet, though a narrow edge of white showed beneath his cloak.

“Yes.”

Ultimos drew the gray cloak closed over his armor.

“The approaches to the Ashward will be observed if your descriptions have spread as widely as she believes. Returning to the healer now would expose her.”

Kisaya folded both arms carefully, hiding the shape beneath her jacket. “So we leave her alone?”

“Until we have a route to remove her if she is discovered. My decision to protect her has not changed.”

Ultimos crossed the loft. Pale morning drew thin lines along his gauntlets as he passed through it.

“The same applies to both of you. Your capture would expose every refuge you know. Leaving you beside the healer would gather all three targets beneath one roof.”

He spoke as if arranging defenses rather than offering protection.

Orin found that more convincing.

“Today,” Ultimos continued, “we identify records that survived outside the Spire. We determine whether the seven-part border around the Order seal appears in any civic plan. We find an approach beneath the Spire and establish a route by which people and anything recovered there can be removed without passing through a Conclave gate.”

Kisaya’s hand had settled over the disk beneath her jacket. Ultimos waited until she lowered it.

“Once the route exists, we return to those who preserved the white art.”

The fingers of one gauntlet closed, then opened. Nema’s hidden door lay beneath the roofs to the south.

“The question is whether the Conclave leaves us time.”

The qualification sounded as though he resented it.

Orin pushed himself upright. “The Civic Survey Hall keeps property maps, construction plans, and demolition orders. Public records won’t show a secret entrance, but they might show what the Spire was built over. If the disk belonged to a city structure, its mark might survive in a survey legend.”

Orin had carried repair permits to the Hall for Ressa, their roofing forewoman, often enough to know that ordinary surveys were viewed without recording the reader’s name. He had even copied the requirements for the junior records examination once, though he had never been able to pay the application fee. Kisaya knew the eastern delivery court because she had hauled slate through it for Ressa’s crew.

Neither knew how many guards watched it.

“Then we assume every entrance is watched,” Ultimos said.

He continued until he understood how the records were arranged and how quickly the building could close. Where the children lacked an answer, he marked the uncertainty and moved on. It felt less like making a plan with him than being fitted into one.

When he finished, Kisaya surveyed Orin’s dye-stained coat and single boot. “The entire south market has that description.”

Between the loft’s small cache and the abandoned workers’ lockers below, they assembled a moth-eaten coat, two caps, a green scarf, and one mismatched boot two sizes too large.

Kisaya packed the boot’s toe with folded cloth, then found a length of glazing cord. She passed it beneath the arch, crossed it over Orin’s instep, and wound it twice around his ankle.

Her fingers missed the final loop. Kisaya stared at the loose cord, untied the whole thing, and began again as though Orin had not seen. The second roofer’s hitch held.

Orin looked down at it. “You tied the boot to me.”

“That is generally how boots work.”

He took several steps. The oversized toe struck first, but the heel no longer lifted.

While Kisaya worked, Ultimos searched the glassworks storage rooms. He returned carrying a slate-gray employee’s cloak, stiff with dust but otherwise intact. A faded glassworks mark remained stitched inside its collar.

Kisaya hid her braid beneath the canvas cap and wrapped the green scarf around her injured arm. Orin pulled the moth-eaten coat over his purple-stained one, and the felt cap buried his blond hair beneath its brim.

The work cloak had been cut broad enough to cover a glassworker’s heavy apron. Ultimos drew his soot-darkened cloak close around the armor, fastened the employee’s garment over it, and raised its deep hood across the white helmet. He remained too tall, straight, and still to be ordinary, but no longer resembled a white statue hiding beneath soot.

Kisaya circled him once. “You almost look as if you belong here.”

“I did.”

He stepped toward the ladder.

Orin followed before Kisaya found another question.

They left the glassworks separately.

Kisaya crossed the lane first with a laborer’s irritated stride. Orin followed with a dusty wooden glazing template under one arm. Ultimos joined them only after they had turned twice through the morning crowds.

The black mages had given the search to the city.

Wanted notices covered a wall near the dye market. The drawings were poor, but the words beneath them were not: BLOND MALE, APPROXIMATELY FIFTEEN. BLACK-HAIRED FEMALE, APPROXIMATELY SEVENTEEN. WITNESSES SOUGHT IN CIVILIAN MURDER. POSSIBLY ASSOCIATED WITH THE SOUTHERN MAGICAL DISTURBANCE.

Orin read the notice twice.

“Yesterday they tried to kill us for witnessing that murder. Now they’re asking the city to find us because of it.”

“Those three never returned,” Kisaya said. “Their commanders may not know they’re dead, but they know they disappeared near the Meridian—and half the market saw us running there.”

“So they call us witnesses.”

“People hide fugitives. They point out witnesses.” Her hand settled over the disk beneath her jacket. “This lets them search for us without telling anyone what their mages were really recovering.”

Ultimos studied the paper without slowing, then continued walking.

They climbed out of the Ashward without crossing a wall. Mud became fitted stone, open drains vanished beneath engraved grates, and public fountains cleaned their own basins. Black magic became quieter as it became more expensive: heat moved behind tiled walls, lifts climbed on braided air, and water ran uphill without a mage in sight.

Ultimos tracked every spell they passed. He slowed when one branch of a glassblower’s blue flame pulsed behind the others, but continued without comment.

“Nothing?” Kisaya asked.

“It remains inefficient.”

The Civic Survey Hall occupied a broad stone building in the Black Spire’s shadow. Its pale, square lower walls were older than the twisted black additions above them. Iron bridges joined narrow rooftop towers to neighboring ministries, carrying a stream of dark-coated clerks and paper.

Ultimos stopped at the edge of the avenue.

“The lower structure predates the Liberation,” he said.

“How can you tell?” Orin asked.

“I approved its eastern foundation.”

The public entrance was unusable. Two clerks sat beneath copies of the wanted notice, entering every visitor’s name and district in a ledger while a silver-chained guard made each one lower hats, scarves, and hoods. Orin watched a mason protest until the guard turned his face toward the light and compared it with the drawings on the wall.

Kisaya led them around the eastern side of the building.

The outer delivery door stood open. Beyond it, an inspection table and iron wicket separated the entrance from the inner court.

Kisaya had remembered the court, but not the silver-chained guard now seated inside it. A bored woman studied Orin’s glazing template, Kisaya’s cap, and Ultimos.

“Crew?” she asked.

Orin’s mind emptied.

Kisaya dropped the template onto the inspection table.

“Third-floor glazing,” she said. “Floor steward says the opening doesn’t match the plan.”

“Work chit?”

“With the floor steward who rejected it. If he’d sent the chit back with us, we wouldn’t be standing here.”

The guard pressed one thumb against the template’s corner joint, then shifted her gaze to Ultimos.

“He with you?”

“Do you want to tell him the measurements were wrong?” Kisaya asked.

Ultimos stood in complete silence.

The guard considered this, then opened a ledger.

“Three laborers. Third-floor glazing.” She wrote the words in a narrow column and pushed a numbered clay token across the table. “Return that when you leave. If it’s still out at shift change, the floor steward gets a visit.”

Orin took the token.

The guard did not open the wicket.

“His hood,” she said.

Kisaya’s face did not change. “Kiln took most of what’s under it.”

“Then the drawing won’t match.” The guard looked at Ultimos. “Lower it.”

Ultimos remained still.

The guard’s hand moved toward a brass signal chain beside the ledger.

Ultimos moved once.

His elbow crossed the space between them and struck beneath her cheekbone. The impact made almost no sound. Her knees folded.

Ultimos caught the signal chain with one hand and the guard with the other. He lowered her behind the inspection table before her silver chain could strike the floor.

Orin stared at the place where she had disappeared. “Is she alive?”

“She will wake before the next bell. Nothing is broken.”

Kisaya found a painted slate beneath the table and hooked it across the outer wicket.

DELIVERIES SUSPENDED. INSPECTION IN PROGRESS.

“That will delay the first person who comes through,” she said. “Not the second.”

Ultimos opened the inner wicket. “Then do not waste the delay.”

They left the glazing template beside a stairwell. The service steps climbed past two locked work floors before Kisaya followed a pair of survey laborers through a propped firebreak door. They emerged into the public gallery without returning to the guarded entrance.

“You struck an armed official,” Orin whispered.

“She was calling others,” Ultimos said.

“That explains why. It doesn’t make me like it.”

“I did not require you to.”

Kisaya glanced back toward the service stairs. “Argue while walking.”

Ultimos led them down the corridor as though he had always intended to enter that way.

The public survey room was almost disappointingly ordinary.

Wooden tables filled a tall-windowed hall. Numbered clay tubes crowded shelves from floor to ceiling. Dust softened everything. Builders argued over boundaries while a landlord in green silk accused a drainage plan of personal betrayal and three clerks ignored him with professional calm.

No magical locks guarded the shelves, and no black fire waited to expose forbidden intentions.

At the front desk, Orin exchanged one of Kisaya’s copper pieces for a table token and a stick of charcoal. The clerk pushed a request slate toward him without looking up.

“District and parcel.”

Ultimos leaned over the slate.

“The Eastern Court. Seventh avenue, below the Meridian road.”

The clerk looked up.

“There has not been a Meridian road in the survey index for three hundred years.”

Orin took the charcoal from him.

“Old South Fourteen,” he said quickly. “Foundation surveys and water routes.”

“Where did you find the old name?”

“On a repair order. The master wants to know whether the old foundations cross the present water line.”

The clerk’s attention lingered on Ultimos’s hood.

“South Fourteen was divided thirty years ago.”

“Demolition index first,” Orin said. “We need the prior parcel numbers.”

That sounded sufficiently tedious to be legitimate.

The clerk pointed them toward a shelf. Instead of wiping the request slate for its next use, he placed it beside his elbow.

They divided the search without discussing how. Ultimos supplied names the city had erased and recognized old foundations beneath later revisions. Orin followed parcel numbers through demolition indexes. Kisaya searched newer permits for structures workers had been ordered not to touch.

Separately, each trail ended. Laid across one another, they began to form a map.

Orin learned how thoroughly a city could bury its past without destroying a single record. The Eastern Court had become a confiscated ceremonial estate. A healing sanctuary survived as an exclusive medical storehouse. Roads Ultimos named had been divided into parcels and renamed after the archmages credited with replacing them.

Ultimos found roof supports carrying the weight of an omitted western hall. Orin traced its abandoned parcel number through two demolition indexes until it ended beside a condemned fire route. Kisaya produced a later permit for the same space: AUTHORIZED SUBLEVEL. NO EXCAVATION. A faded catalog number had been copied into its lower margin.

Orin laid the permit beside the demolition index. The numbers matched except for the fire-route prefix.

“This wasn’t filed with the foundational surveys,” he said. “Someone moved it into the fire collection and left the old parcel number behind.”

Orin requested the tube by its fire-route number.

An elderly clerk brought the tube to their table. A faded black cord bound the clay.

“Foundational survey,” he said. “Don’t breathe hard near it.”

The tube held a thin sheet of treated cloth, yellowed at the folds and repaired so many times that newer thread covered much of its edge.

The labels had been written over.

The lines beneath remained.

Ultimos braced both hands against the table without touching the cloth. The old survey crackled softly as Orin opened its final fold.

Seven towers stood along the southern rise. A broad court opened east of them. Beneath the streets, narrow double lines connected administrative halls, sanctuaries, reservoirs, and a circular structure outside the old wall.

Orin recognized the road descending toward that circle.

The Meridian temple.

The original survey left it unnamed. A later hand had reduced it to two words in the margin: FOUNDATION VOID.

“This passage,” Ultimos said.

His finger followed a double line north from the eastern administrative quarter.

“It carried instructors and civic masters between the court and the central halls during emergency closure. There were access chambers beneath each third crossing.”

The passage ended near the center of old Veyr.

The modern Black Spire had not existed when the survey was drawn. Neither had the pumping house, the expanded northern wall, or any street Orin could name.

“Where is the Spire’s original foundation plan?” Ultimos asked.

No original foundation plan existed. The index offered repairs, reinforcements, and authorized sublevels, but every public record began with the tower already standing.

Orin returned with a current drainage survey and the earliest available plan of the Spire’s lower supports. He spread both beside the treated cloth.

They did not fit. Different scales, shifted streets, and an expanded wall made the plans useless until the three of them found points shared by both cities.

Ultimos supplied the first fixed point. He touched the eastern foundation of the building around them, drawn on the old cloth as part of an administrative hall.

“This has not moved.”

Orin found the second in a demolition permit. The Eastern Court’s reflecting pool survived beneath a public bath, its curved western edge preserved because later builders had used it as a cistern wall.

Kisaya found the third. Modern surveyors had divided the old covered aqueduct into disconnected inspection shafts, but she had crossed the roofline above those shafts often enough to recognize the curve they made together.

Orin marked intervals along the edge of a discarded request slip and used them to carry distances between the two scales. He turned the drainage survey, fixed the three shared points in charcoal, and tried again.

The lines approached one another.

Not perfectly.

They could place the old passage only within a strip of modern Veyr nearly thirty paces wide.

“How close is that?” Kisaya asked.

“Thirty paces, if the later surveyors measured the cistern wall from the same edge.”

“That is not close underground.”

“It is closer than an entire city.”

They had not found a map of what lay beneath the Spire. They had built one from records the city had never expected anyone to place together.

Within the thirty-pace strip, the Spire’s foundation bent around a long maintenance void.

“They built over the passage,” Orin said.

“Around it,” Ultimos corrected. “They found something they could not remove.”

Orin followed the void north until it crossed the storm-water system near the old pumping house.

Kisaya leaned over the table. “That drain still exists.”

She tapped a runoff branch that disappeared beneath the reservoir road. “There’s a mouth behind the north wheel. Children use it to reach the canal.”

On the ancient survey, the passage continued toward waterworks buried against the former northern wall. The modern plan showed a sealed reservoir and overflow on the same alignment.

“If the connection remains continuous,” Ultimos said, “the old waterworks may provide an outer entrance to the network, away from the Spire’s central approaches.”

“And the reservoir reaches the outer wall,” Kisaya said. “So it might be a way out.”

“An extraction route.”

“That sounds like a way out wearing armor.”

“A retreat need only carry those who entered. I intend to remove records, artifacts, and prisoners, if any remain.”

Kisaya’s expression lost its brief amusement.

Orin copied the route while Ultimos committed it to memory.

Kisaya’s finger stopped near the legend of the ancient survey.

Among symbols for cisterns, public halls, and structural supports, the White Order seal appeared inside a double ring broken at seven points. It had been added in darker ink than the original legend and marked four circular chambers along the underground network. One was the Meridian temple. Another lay beneath the Spire.

Beside the symbol, an older word had been scraped away. Later ink supplied a bland instruction:

ACCESS CLOSED. NO EXCAVATION.

“Orin.”

He saw the seven divisions and moved the slip to the edge of the table, screening Kisaya’s hands from the nearest clerk.

She eased the disk from beneath her jacket.

The symbol on its face matched the center of the legend. Its seven notches matched the breaks in the outer ring, and every hooked finger and angled ray was identical.

Ultimos stepped into the narrow space beside them before either child called him.

“The Order seal belongs to the central figure,” he said. “The outer ring is not a notation I know.”

Orin’s skin prickled. “It marks four of them.”

Kisaya looked at Ultimos. “What stood there?”

One gauntleted finger moved from one circular point to the next without touching the cloth.

“Inspection chambers. Nothing that required a seal when I last used these passages.”

“Then someone changed them after you were imprisoned,” Orin said.

“Yes.”

It was a small word. Ultimos made it sound like a threat directed across four centuries.

Kisaya turned the disk over beneath the request slip. “So it opens them.”

“It may identify a bearer, authorize passage, complete a mechanism, or serve a purpose none of us has named.” Ultimos indicated the disk with two fingers. “We will not discover which by pressing it against the first closed structure we find.”

“The last one had you inside it,” she said.

“Which is why ignorance will not decide what we release next.”

A single bell sounded from the lower court.

Ultimos lifted his head.

“The guard is awake,” he said.

Kisaya concealed the disk while Orin copied the four chamber locations onto the back of the slip. He returned the fragile survey to its tube and left the modern plans beneath their weights. Only when he released the charcoal did he realize that the work had silenced the chamber in his head.

The black feather began falling again.

He stood before it reached the floor.

No alarm bells rang. Not yet.

At the front desk, Orin set down the table token. The elderly clerk looked from it to the charcoal dust across their table, but the three of them were already past him.

The numbered delivery token pressed against Orin’s thigh. Behind them waited the guard’s ledger, the abandoned glazing template, and the request slate beside the clerk’s elbow. They had left a trail so clean he could almost hear boots following it.

They crossed the public room with their reconstructed route divided between Orin’s sleeve and their memories: a probable approach beneath the Spire, a drain that might intersect it, and an old waterworks that might reach beyond the northern wall.

The exit opened into the Hall of Liberation.

The Hall formed a vast rotunda between several civic offices. Black stone columns rose through three levels of balconies. Sunlight poured from a glass dome high above, caught in suspended spheres of water and flame, and broke across the floor in blue and gold.

People crossed beneath it without slowing.

Clerks carried bundles between ministries. Academy students clustered near the stairs. A teacher pointed out the names of the first archmages engraved around the dome while six children tried unsuccessfully to appear interested.

Liberation art covered every wall.

One relief showed black mages opening a locked academy to waiting citizens. Another depicted the Grasping Hand withdrawing from fields of grain. White-robed figures watched from balconies with narrow faces and empty eyes.

Ultimos passed them.

His pace did not change.

Orin noticed each one because Ultimos did not.

They were halfway across the rotunda when the white mage stopped.

The mural occupied the entire northern wall.

It began at floor level and climbed beyond the second balcony, wide enough that the figures nearest its center stood three times taller than living men. Centuries of varnish had darkened its edges, but restoration scaffolds flanked one side, and the colors beneath the dome remained painfully bright.

At the center stood a man in white armor.

A red cross divided his featureless helmet.

His hands were raised over a crowd of civilians. Enormous white lines descended around them in intersecting planes, forming a cage of radiant geometry. At the top of the lattice, those planes met the lower edge of a stone gate leaning inward over the crowd. Behind the white mage, ranks of pale-armored soldiers crowded the failing entrance. Roofs split. Towers fell. The painted sky burned with the last battle of an age.

Opposite the white mage, a black-robed archmage drove a staff through the cage. The pale lines broke before him. Civilians reached toward the opening while black mages shielded them from falling stone.

Gold letters crossed the base.

THE LIBERATION OF THE EASTERN GATE

ARCHMAGE CAEDRAN BREAKS THE PRISON-LATTICE OF THE LAST WHITE LORD AND RETURNS THREE HUNDRED SOULS TO FREEDOM.

Orin knew the story. In the school primers available to the Ashward, the white mage inside it had never possessed a name.

Those books described the lattice as a civic binding ritual raised by scores of servants over days—a static ward for confining neighborhoods, not a spell one man could cast amid a collapsing battle.

Orin had seen that geometry from the inside.

When the Warden turned his fire upon him and Kisaya, intersecting planes like those had curved around them. The flames had broken outward.

In the mural, the gate was falling inward.

The lattice was not shaped to keep the crowd from escaping. It was shaped to keep the gate from crushing them.

The man beside him gave the nameless figure a name.

Ultimos stood below his painted likeness, his shoulders square to the gate. The slate-gray work cloak concealed the armor, the hood buried the cross, and the mural’s proportions were heroic and grotesque. None of it concealed the angle of the helmet or the breadth of the pauldrons beneath the borrowed cloth.

The mural had waited four hundred years for the man it condemned to stand beneath it.
