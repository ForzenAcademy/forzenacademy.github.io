Volume One · Chapter Nine

The Name That Returned

Ultimos

The first person to approach Ultimos was carrying a dead child.

He came from the northwestern collapse with the girl held against his chest and one of her bare feet swinging beneath his arm. Brick dust had turned his hair gray. Blood covered the front of his shirt, though Orin could not tell how much belonged to him.

The man crossed the flooded junction without looking at the vanished Warden formations. He did not look at Solmir inside the white enclosure or at the surviving officers gathered beyond the streets.

He stopped in front of the red cross.

“You healed the burned boy.”

Ultimos looked down at what he carried.

The girl could not have been older than eight. A roof tile had opened the side of her head above one ear. Rainwater had thinned the blood into pale red lines across her cheek.

Ultimos touched two fingers to her throat.

Orin knew the answer before he withdrew them.

“Her living continuity has ended,” Ultimos said.

The father adjusted his hold as though the child had merely slipped. “Then start it again.”

“I cannot.”

“You put hands back together.” His voice rose. “They said you made a whole wall remember where it belonged.”

“Stone does not die.”

The man stared at him.

Ultimos did not soften the truth. He did not offer the comfort people usually built around words that could not be changed.

The father looked past the white armor toward the broken command post.

“You did this.”

Rain ran from the child’s toes into the water covering the road.

“Yes,” Ultimos said.

The man struck him.

His fist hit the cracked breastplate and broke two knuckles with a sound Orin heard from ten paces away. The father folded around his hand. The girl never moved.

Ultimos caught them both before they fell.

He lowered the child to a dry section of the shattered monument and closed pale geometry around the father’s fingers. Bone returned to alignment. Split skin joined. The restoration took seconds.

The man pulled his healed hand away as though Ultimos had burned it.

He lifted his daughter and carried her into the rain.

No one tried to stop him.

The old gatehouse junction filled slowly after that.

People emerged from the chain pits, alleys, workshops, and buildings whose walls still rested on trembling constructions of light. Some came to search for family. Others brought doors, blankets, broken tables, and anything flat enough to carry someone who could no longer walk.

The Wardens who had surrendered did not resume their formation. They worked beside Ashward laborers without orders. A fire mage climbed onto a roof with an ordinary bucket. Two earth workers used pry bars because their portable anchors lay cut apart in the street. The older wind Warden had recovered her discarded focus, but she kept it fastened shut while she directed people away from unstable walls.

Nobody asked who had won.

Kisaya stood beside Orin with one hand closed around her injured arm.

She had not spoken since Ultimos’s declaration.

Across the junction, the father disappeared beneath a cooperage awning. Someone placed a coat over the girl. Her bare foot remained visible for several breaths until the cloth was adjusted.

“He told the truth,” Orin said.

Kisaya looked at him.

He had not meant to defend Ultimos. The words had come from the same useless place that made people explain the direction of rain.

“I know,” she said.

That was worse.

A woman cried out near the collapsed command post.

Orin turned.

The person with the ruined leg had stopped breathing.

The Warden medic opened the red case again. Her hands moved quickly through bottles and folded cloth, then stopped over choices that could no longer help. She pressed both palms against the injured person’s chest and shouted for another medic.

Nobody answered.

Ultimos crossed the junction.

The crowd moved away from him before understanding where he was going. A laborer abandoned one end of a stretcher. A surviving Warden reached for an empty belt where his focus had been, then remembered it lay on the road behind him.

Ultimos knelt beside the injured person.

“The chest,” the medic said. Fear competed with training in her face. Training won by a narrow margin. “Collapsed lung. Maybe blood around the heart. I can’t tell.”

Pale lines passed through wet cloth and skin.

“Both,” Ultimos said.

His damaged gauntlet settled over the sternum. Fine geometry entered the body beneath it, tracing relationships Orin could not see. The injured person convulsed once. Blood ran backward across the opened trouser seam. The crushed leg lengthened as broken pieces found one another beneath the skin.

Ultimos stopped.

The person drew a wet, tearing breath.

The medic pressed two fingers to the throat, then stared at the white helmet.

“Why did you stop?”

“Three vessels remain damaged. The heart is stable.” Light flickered at the split in Ultimos’s left pauldron. “My precision is insufficient while I maintain the structures around us.”

The medic looked at the pale supports holding two buildings above the street.

“If I open the leg, can you show me where?”

“Yes.”

She took a knife from the red case.

That became the beginning of the treatment line.

It formed without anyone deciding where it should go. The wounded were carried toward the center because the white mage had knelt there. Wardens brought civilians. Civilians brought Wardens. The medical officer cut cloth, assigned pressure, and sent people with minor injuries toward the cooperage.

Ultimos did not apologize for any of them.

He restored what remained possible.

A boy with glass in his abdomen kept enough blood to survive the removal. A fire worker’s ruined hand recovered movement, though the burn scars remained because Ultimos released the pattern before finishing the surface. He closed an old woman’s torn artery and left two laborers to bind the wound. When a Warden with half his face burned was placed before him, the red cross lowered without hesitation.

The man woke screaming.

Ultimos continued until both eyes opened.

Orin watched the same hands that had removed entire formations return a stranger’s eyelid to its proper curve.

Nothing in the movements contradicted anything else.

That realization frightened him more than inconsistency would have.

Ultimos was not trying to become kind again. Broken bodies and hostile formations simply demanded different corrections.

“Orin.”

Kisaya was looking toward the southern street.

Sela had returned.

She leaned on the older wind Warden and limped around the abandoned earth anchor. Her restraints were gone, but red bands remained around her wrists. Beside her walked a small woman carrying a brown medical case twice the size of the Warden’s red one.

Nema had not bothered hiding her gray hair beneath its usual scarf. One side of her dress had been hastily pinned above the mud. She surveyed the junction, the treatment line, and the spaces where there were no bodies to treat.

Then she saw Ultimos.

Her mouth tightened.

“You found her,” Orin said.

Sela’s injured foot nearly failed beneath her. “People were coming down Furnace Lane asking for Nema. There wasn’t much secret left to protect.”

Nema set her case beside the first stretcher.

“How many healers?” she asked the Warden medic.

“Three alive. Two can still work. One has a broken arm.”

“Doctors?”

“West Clinic is behind containment. We sent runners.”

“Send them again. Tell the clinic to bring surgeons, boiled cloth, and every chest tube they own. If they ask who authorized the expense, tell them Archmage Solmir did.”

Inside his enclosure, Solmir looked toward her.

Nema met his eyes.

“He can object when he stops being a public decoration.”

The Warden medic made a startled sound. It almost became a laugh and seemed to hurt when it did.

Nema opened her case.

She had brought needles, wrapped blades, splints, dried herbs, two cloudy bottles, and the small iron kettle Orin had seen above her treatment-room stove. Nothing inside could serve a junction full of casualties for long.

She began anyway.

Ultimos finished stabilizing the burned Warden and rose.

Nema pointed to a woman whose breathing had become shallow. “That one next.”

The red cross faced her.

“Unless being ordered by someone without a title damages your concentration.”

Ultimos went to the woman.

Nema worked opposite him.

She did not treat him as a miracle. She checked every restoration he left incomplete, packed wounds after he stopped internal bleeding, and made the Warden medic mark people who could wait. Twice she told him not to spend precision on scarring. Once she slapped his hand away from a man whose pupils had already fixed.

“You cannot recover him,” she said.

“I am aware.”

“Then use that time on someone you can.”

He moved.

Kisaya watched them from beside the shattered monument.

“She’s angry enough to survive anything,” she said.

Orin wanted to agree.

The Black Spire rose above the roofs beyond Nema’s shoulder.

Its crooked towers and hooked battlements had disappeared into low cloud. Red signals moved through the gaps in its walls, visible for an instant and gone behind another impossible angle. The light that had escaped Ultimos’s attack had reached it.

The city’s alarms had stopped.

That silence worried Orin more.

Inside the white enclosure, Solmir had not moved from the damaged northern wall.

His boot still covered the fracture at its base.

Orin remembered seeing it after the command station fell.

He crossed the flooded street.

Solmir noticed him before Ultimos did.

“You read civic signals,” the Archmage said.

Orin stopped outside the pale wall. “Sometimes.”

“What do the lamps say now?”

Most of the lamps in the junction were broken. The one above the western lane held no flame, but a thin black line had returned inside its lower channel.

It did not pulse toward the Ashward.

It drew steadily north.

“Nothing,” Orin said.

Solmir’s expression changed by less than a smile.

Orin looked at the boot over the cracked anchor.

“Move your foot.”

“You may order the Archmage when your friend finishes removing the government around him.”

Orin stepped closer to the enclosure.

The light at its base trembled. Solmir’s sole had hidden most of the fracture, but something dark showed beneath its edge: a thin piece of black metal driven into the gap.

One of the fasteners from his ruined command cord.

Solmir had been working it into the anchor while everyone watched Ultimos.

“Ultimos!”

The black line inside the western lamp went out.

Every surviving street lamp opened at once.

No fire appeared.

The glass fronts folded inward and exposed silver mirrors behind them. Each mirror turned toward the treatment line.

Ultimos rose from beside his patient.

“Down,” he said.

Darkness crossed the junction.

It came as six broad sheets thrown from the roofs, heavy enough to strike like falling canvas. Pale light cut through two before they landed. The others opened above the wounded.

They were not cloth.

Fine black powder poured from them.

Orin covered his mouth. The powder entered his eyes and turned the junction into a field of gray shapes. People coughed. Someone knocked over Nema’s kettle. The rain caught the dust and changed it into clinging paste across skin, stone, and spell light.

“Marking ash,” the older wind Warden shouted. “Do not cast through it!”

The Warden medic froze with her focus halfway open.

Orin had seen marking ash used after warehouse fires. It clung to active elemental paths and made hidden workings visible for investigators. Enough of it could blind the caster with the light of every connection at once.

It was not made for white magic.

It did not need to be.

Ultimos maintained the supports, Solmir’s enclosure, and half a dozen unfinished restorations. Every pale relationship around him ignited beneath the ash.

The junction became white.

Orin saw the lines holding roofs, walls, bodies, and the Archmage’s prison converge upon the armored figure at their center. There were too many to follow. The sight vanished into radiance.

Ultimos closed one hand.

Wind erupted beneath the pale plane over the chain pit.

The older Warden had opened her focus despite the ash. She forced the powder upward in a single column while silver fire raced along her sleeve, revealing every current she controlled.

“Move the wounded!” she shouted.

People dragged stretchers beneath the cooperage awning.

Something struck the northern street.

Wheels screamed against wet paving.

A black carriage broke through the abandoned barrier without horses. Wind drove it from behind in a compressed wall, then vanished. The vehicle continued on ordinary momentum, armored sides shedding brick as it entered the junction.

Three more followed.

No lamps announced them. No command seals opened above their roofs. The people riding the running boards wore gray coats without civic chains, rank cords, or academy marks.

Their faces were covered by flat black masks.

The first carriage turned across the northern approach and stopped between Ultimos and the Spire. Its rear doors opened before the wheels settled.

Warders jumped down carrying crossbows.

Not spell foci.

Crossbows.

The first volley struck the pale supports beneath the northwestern building.

Each bolt carried a glass bulb. The bulbs broke against the light and released more marking ash directly into its geometry. White lines flared. One support disappeared.

The building shifted.

Ultimos turned toward it.

The second volley struck Solmir’s enclosure.

Most bolts shattered against the walls. One passed through the fracture at the base, hit the metal fastener beneath Solmir’s boot, and drove it deeper.

The northern anchor broke.

One wall of the prison folded inward.

Solmir was already moving.

He threw himself through the opening as the remaining planes tried to close around him. A masked warder caught his coat and dragged him clear. Another snapped a black band around his wrist—not a restraint, Orin realized, but a ward prepared to accept his magic.

Fire entered Solmir’s hand.

He cast it into the street.

The flame did not attack Ultimos. It spread in a low line beneath the stretchers and divided the treatment area from the pale supports holding the buildings.

People surged away from it.

The extraction force had learned the same lesson as Solmir.

They did not attack the white mage.

They attacked everything he had chosen to keep alive.

Ultimos opened both hands.

The fire lost its structure, but burning oil remained across the paving. A rigid plane pushed it away from the wounded. At the same time, new light replaced the support that had failed beneath the northwestern wall.

The masked warders reached Nema.

She drove a medical knife through the back of the first man’s hand.

He shouted. Nema twisted the blade free and struck his mask with the iron kettle.

The second warder caught her from behind.

“Practitioner confirmed,” he said. “Take her.”

Kisaya moved before Orin understood the words.

She crossed the broken monument, ducked beneath a crossbow, and threw her shoulder into the warder holding Nema. All three hit the road. Nema clawed for her case. The warder with the injured hand kicked it away.

Glass bottles broke across the paving.

“Leave her!” Sela shouted.

She limped into the fight and struck the masked man with a length of restraint chain.

A third warder raised his crossbow toward her.

Orin seized the weapon from the side.

The string released.

The bolt passed through his coat beneath one arm and buried itself in the monument. Marking ash exploded across the carved black archmage.

The warder drove an elbow into Orin’s face.

Light flashed behind his eyes. He lost his grip and fell into the water.

Kisaya had reached Nema’s wrist.

“Run!”

“With which leg?” Nema snapped.

She was not injured. The person beneath her was.

During the struggle, one stretcher had overturned beside the fire. A young Warden lay on the road with a glass shard driven beneath her ribs. Blood pulsed around it each time she tried to breathe.

Nema had one hand pressed over the wound.

The familiar three-curve pattern struggled above her fingers.

Weak white light entered the Warden’s side.

Every masked face turned toward it.

Solmir saw from the open carriage.

“Alive,” he ordered. “The healer comes alive.”

Nema looked up at him.

“You threatened one patient to find me,” she said. “How many did you bury to escape?”

Solmir’s face tightened.

He did not answer.

The warder with the wounded hand pulled Kisaya away by her injured arm.

She screamed.

Ultimos crossed the junction.

The marking ash exposed the lines gathering around him. A straight construction began at his right palm.

Every masked warder near Nema stopped.

They had seen what those lines meant.

The man holding Kisaya released her.

The one behind Nema pressed a short iron blade against her throat.

“End the construction.”

Ultimos continued forward.

“He will kill you too,” Solmir said from the carriage.

Nema did not look away from the red cross.

“Will you?” she asked.

The pale line extended another handspan.

Orin pushed himself upright. Blood ran from his nose into his mouth. He could see the path of the forming weapon.

It passed through the warder’s chest.

And Nema’s.

“Ultimos,” he said.

The white line disappeared.

It did not flicker or fail.

Ultimos chose to end it.

The masked warders moved at once.

One locked black bands around Nema’s wrists. Another threw her medical case into the carriage. The man with the blade dragged her backward without taking the edge from her throat.

Kisaya lunged again.

Nema kicked her.

The heel struck Kisaya squarely in the chest and sent her into Sela’s arms.

“Do not make me the next excuse!” Nema shouted.

The warders pulled her into the carriage.

The words reached Ultimos.

Orin saw them reach him in the slight angle of the helmet, the same way a blade might find a join too small for anyone else to notice.

The northwestern building shifted again.

Marking-ash bolts had extinguished two supports. A whole corner descended toward the treatment line.

Ultimos stopped.

He faced the carriage carrying Solmir and Nema.

Then he turned his back on it.

White frames opened beneath the falling building.

The first caught the upper floor. The second closed around a cracked foundation pier. The third spread above the wounded as brick and slate poured across its surface.

The carriages began to move.

Wind struck them from behind, accelerated each vehicle through the northern street, and released before Ultimos could seize an active path. Iron wheels carried them beyond the old gatehouse.

The older Warden raised a current to stop the last carriage.

A masked rider dropped a hooked cable behind it. The hook caught her focus chain and pulled her off her feet. She released the spell before the wheels dragged her beneath them.

Kisaya ran after Nema.

Orin caught her around the waist.

She hit him once. The second blow lost strength. By the third she was only trying to move forward while he held on with both arms.

“Let me go!”

“You can’t catch them!”

“Let me go!”

The first carriage reached the end of the northern lane.

Black wings unfolded from its sides.

They were canvas over iron frames, broad enough to span the street. Wind returned beneath them and lifted the wheels from the paving. The following carriages opened their wings in order. All three rose between the roofs, climbed above the Ashward, and turned toward the Black Spire.

Ultimos held the building.

Pale light covered both hands. The marking ash made every trembling line visible from street to roof. If he released the construction, the treatment line would disappear beneath three floors of brick.

If he carried himself into the air through white magic, he could maintain no other spell. The supports would fail before he reached the end of the street.

He watched the carriages climb.

The damaged red cross followed them until low cloud swallowed their wings.

Then the Black Spire took them.

Its crooked silhouette opened around the flying carriages in ways distance could not explain. Towers overlapped. Hooked roofs hid one another. A black gate appeared between two walls that had looked separate a moment earlier.

The carriages entered.

The gate became part of the Spire again.

Kisaya stopped fighting Orin.

She stood in the flooded street with his arms still around her and stared at the place where Nema had vanished.

“We brought him to her,” she said.

Orin loosened his hold.

“I know.”

“We brought all of this to her.”

There was no answer that did not make the truth smaller.

Sela bent beside the young Warden Nema had refused to abandon. The three pale curves had faded, but the bleeding had slowed. The Warden medic reached them and pressed folded cloth around the glass shard.

“She saved her,” Sela said.

Kisaya looked toward Ultimos.

“Then save Nema.”

The northwestern building settled onto the new frames.

Ultimos closed each construction into the surviving foundation before lowering his hands. The last lines dimmed but remained. Rain and marking ash had turned his white armor gray. The fractured pauldron hung open, and the burn in his breastplate reached almost to the red cross above it.

He crossed the junction.

People moved aside.

Kisaya did not.

“You watched them take her.”

Ultimos stopped in front of her.

“I watched their route, their protections, the intervals at which they released propulsion, and the gate that admitted them.”

“That isn’t what I said.”

“If I had followed, the building would have killed everyone beneath it. If I had fired, the carriage would have fallen with Nema inside. If I had attempted to seize its protected form while sustaining the supports, both efforts would have failed.”

Kisaya’s hands closed.

“You have an answer for everything.”

“No.”

The word ended her next accusation.

Ultimos looked toward the Spire.

“I do not yet have an answer for that tower.”

Its highest points remained hidden in cloud.

The city had offered them conflicting builders, impossible dates, and public plans that began after the structure was already standing. Now it had swallowed two people through a gate that appeared only when approached from the air.

Orin wiped blood from his mouth.

“They’ll interrogate Nema.”

“Yes.”

“They know what she can do.”

“Yes.”

“And Solmir knows she matters to you.”

The featureless helmet turned toward him.

Orin made himself continue.

“Maybe not to you the way she matters to us. But he watched you listen to her. He watched her use white magic beside you. He knows she has part of what you came to recover.”

“He also knows I refused to destroy her in order to prevent her capture.”

Kisaya’s face hardened. “So she’s another restraint.”

“She is a practitioner of my Order held by the institution that criminalized her inheritance.” Ultimos’s voice remained level. “She is not a restraint. She is an objective.”

Something in the words returned a little air to Orin’s lungs.

It did not make Ultimos safer.

It made his direction clear.

“You were already going into the Spire,” Orin said.

“Yes.”

“Now Solmir is there. Nema is there. The archive is there.”

“And the authorities have received enough evidence to begin destroying what they cannot defend.”

Ultimos looked down at Kisaya’s jacket.

“Show me the disk.”

Kisaya’s hand moved over it.

For a breath, the distrust of the ruins returned. The artifact was the one thing they possessed that might matter to Ultimos as much as his own plans. Giving him access to it had once seemed like surrendering their only leverage.

Then Nema disappeared into the clouds above them.

Kisaya drew out the pale disk.

Rainwater ran across its double ring and the open hand surrounded by rays. One of the seven interruptions along its edge held a weak white glow.

Orin had seen that light beneath the northern reservoir.

This time it did not circle the disk.

It remained fixed toward the Black Spire.

Ultimos did not take it.

He extended two fingers close enough for pale perception lines to cross the surface. Geometry appeared inside the outer ring, finer than the marks visible in ordinary light. Several paths resembled the service plate beneath the reservoir. Others continued beyond it and converged upon an arrangement Orin remembered from the Civic Survey Hall.

Four recognition points.

Every one had been drawn around the same empty center.

“It answered the passage under the northern waterworks,” Orin said.

“A subordinate relay,” Ultimos replied.

His fingers shifted. The disk’s fixed light sharpened toward the tower.

“The credential is seeking the office that issued it.”

Kisaya stared at the Spire. “Your office?”

“One of the central authorities of old Veyr. I do not recognize this seal closely enough to name which.”

“But it points there.”

“Something beneath the Spire recognizes it.”

The northern outlet had answered the disk without opening. The four symbols copied from the Civic Survey Hall offered no promise that the buried center would behave differently.

They still did not know what waited there.

They knew where to look.

Kisaya closed her fingers around the artifact.

“Then we use it.”

“We examine it,” Ultimos said. “We identify the corresponding foundation and determine what the modern wards have attached to it. Only then do we use it.”

“Nema doesn’t have time for you to study four centuries of masonry.”

The pale light along Ultimos’s damaged armor brightened.

“Do not mistake preparation for delay.”

His anger had returned to its oldest shape: cold enough to build with.

“The northern route is compromised. The Hall approach is known. Solmir has seen our methods, and the Spire now understands that destroying information may deny it to me.” He looked from the disk to the tower. “We will enter through the foundation they do not admit exists.”

Orin heard the decision settle into place.

“We?”

“You will remain outside once a secure route is established.”

Kisaya laughed once. There was no humor in it.

“You think we’re waiting outside while Nema is in there?”

“I think you lack the training to assault the central authority of Veyr.”

“And you lack the sense to recognize a delivery corridor.”

“Kisaya,” Orin said.

She did not stop.

“You didn’t know which roofs carried people, which pits still connected, or where the lamp signals went. You would have left Sela on that platform because Solmir’s trap made sense to you. You need us.”

Ultimos stood over her in broken white armor.

All around them lay the proof of what happened when his power no longer accepted limits from anyone else.

Kisaya’s voice shook anyway.

“And we need you,” she said. “I hate that. Right now, I hate you a little too. But Nema is in that tower, and neither of us gets what we want without the other.”

Orin waited for Ultimos to rule the argument finished.

The red cross turned from Kisaya to him.

“Do you share this position?”

Orin looked at the dead beneath coats, the injured beneath white supports, and the Spire above all of it.

He remembered the Warden who carried him through floodwater. He remembered Ultimos healing that same uniform after erasing others. He remembered Nema refusing to abandon a wounded stranger when escape stood within reach.

“Yes.”

Ultimos looked toward the tower again.

“Then survive long enough to be useful.”

It was not permission.

They followed him when he crossed to Solmir’s abandoned staff.

That was how the three of them continued: held together less by trust than by a destination none could reach alone.

The comparison chamber had no windows.

Deep within the Black Spire, wind moved behind the walls without entering the room. Its pressure raised whispers from the shelves and made the iron seals over the cabinets tremble against their locks.

Six officials waited around a table of black stone.

None wore public colors.

The youngest person present was Senior Registrar Pel Arven, who had spent nineteen years earning the authority to open three of the room’s twelve cabinets. Tonight, people with greater authority had ordered him to open nine.

He kept his hands from shaking by recording the damage.

Archmage Solmir sat at one end of the table with dried blood beneath one ear. His mantle was gone. One wrist remained inside a transport ward, and gray marking ash streaked his coat. He had refused a physician until his report was entered.

“The comparison focus?” asked the woman opposite him.

Her chair bore no title. Nine black keys hung from the belt beneath her plain mantle.

“Taken,” Solmir said.

“The image?”

“Not captured.”

“Then your identification failed.”

Solmir placed both hands on the table.

“The test failed. The identification did not.”

Nobody answered immediately.

On the wall behind him, three partial reports repeated inside glass plates.

The first showed a street disappearing through six advancing officers.

The second contained only white radiance, marking ash, and the outline of something armored at the center of too many constructions.

The third had preserved sound.

I will open every seal you closed.

The voice ended in static. Then it returned.

I will recover every person who remains.

Pel had listened to the statements seven times while assembling the records. They had not become less impossible.

The woman with nine keys looked toward him.

“Open the eastern register.”

Pel inserted three keys into the seventh cabinet. Two belonged to him. The third had been removed from a chain around Solmir’s neck.

The locks turned in different directions.

Cold air left the cabinet.

Inside waited a row of white document cases wrapped in black binding wire. They did not resemble the official histories stored elsewhere in the Spire. No Conclave seal covered their pale surfaces. Someone had written classification numbers over older names and failed to scrape the letters beneath them completely away.

Pel selected the case corresponding to the restored face in the Hall mural.

The public catalog named the woman an anonymous subject liberated from White Order control.

The restricted register called her Fermina.

He placed the case on the table.

Inside lay military surveys, confiscation orders, a painted field study, and a narrow strip of linen marked with two names. Fermina’s appeared first. The second had been covered by a black classification stamp.

Pel applied solvent.

Four hundred years of ink loosened reluctantly.

Letters emerged beneath it.

ULTIMOS.

One official left the table.

He walked to the far wall, opened a speaking focus, and ordered every external door of the archive sealed. His voice remained calm until the focus closed.

“A shared name proves nothing,” another said.

Solmir looked at her. “He restored Fermina’s face from memory.”

“The Hall report described artifact-assisted reconstruction.”

“He identified the missing relief gate in an eastern weather confinement. He redirected a master array through a surrender circuit after observing it once. He described the Meridian temples as closures. He called the Severance by name.”

The final word altered the room.

Pel had read it in documents that could not leave this chamber. He had never heard anyone speak it aloud.

The woman with nine keys extended her hand.

“The disposition ledger.”

That required the twelfth cabinet.

Pel did not possess any of its keys.

The six officials supplied them one at a time.

The cabinet opened with a sound like stone breaking under deep water.

There was only one book inside.

Its black cover had been bound around something originally white. The pages listed names Pel recognized from cautionary histories, monuments, and academy lessons. Beside each appeared one of three conclusions.

Confirmed dead.

Unrecovered.

Contained.

Solmir watched him turn to the eastern court entries.

Pel found the name.

The line beside it had never been amended.

ULTIMOS — GRAND WHITE MAGE, EASTERN COURT.

CONTAINED ALIVE: MERIDIAN TEMPLE, LAST FOUNDATION.

SEAL TO REMAIN PERPETUAL.

Beneath the original entry, a later hand had added one final instruction.

NO RECOVERY ATTEMPT AUTHORIZED.

Pel read it twice.

The woman with nine keys took the ledger from him.

“The Last Foundation team?”

“Missing,” said the official at the wall. “Their recovery order was never entered through district command. The assigned seal was removed from custody. The southern event began less than an hour afterward.”

“Removed by whom?”

“The authorization chain is incomplete.”

The woman looked at Solmir. “Did he give you a name?”

“No.”

“Did any witness hear one?”

“Two wanted children left the ruins with him. They are now inside his protection.” Solmir’s mouth tightened around the last word. “The Ashward healer may know.”

Beyond the comparison chamber, metal struck stone on a lower level.

Nema’s voice followed, faint through the walls and furious enough to remain unmistakable.

“If you want that hand to keep working, bring me clean thread.”

Solmir looked toward the wall.

For the first time since entering the chamber, uncertainty crossed his face.

The woman with nine keys closed the ledger.

“Move every Severance record below the seventh level. Separate seal locations from prisoner identities. No single office retains both.”

“And the public account?” someone asked.

“A rogue artifact destroyed the Ashward junction during an attempt upon the Archmage. Casualty lists remain provisional. No reference to white magic is authorized.”

“The district heard him.”

“The district heard many things while its lamps failed.”

She placed one finger over the name on the ledger.

“We have governed four centuries because the dead do not contradict us.”

The recorded voice spoke again from the glass behind her.

The institutions built upon our stolen names will answer for every one.

Nobody in the room suggested it was an artifact.

Nobody called it a construct.

The woman with nine keys looked toward the walls of the Black Spire, as though she could see through their twisting depths to the ancient foundation buried below.

“Find the other seals,” she said. “Before he does.”

Pel stared at the uncovered name.

It had survived beneath black ink, inside a locked cabinet, within a room the city did not know existed. Every public monument insisted the man belonged to an age that had ended. Every official history placed his kind safely beyond reply.

Now the Spire had heard his voice.

In the highest sealed archive of Veyr, the name Ultimos returned to history.

End of Chapter Nine
